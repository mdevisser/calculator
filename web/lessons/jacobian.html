The Jacobian conjecture (dimension ≥ 3)

An 87-year-old guess, broken in dimension 3 and up in July 2026. The two-dimensional case is still open.

The question, in plain words

Start with a recipe that turns one number into another: double it, then add 1. Mathematicians write f(x) = 2x + 1. Feed it 3 and you get 2 × 3 + 1 = 7. Feed it 4 and you get 9.

Inputs 1 apart came out 2 apart. That's true wherever you look: this recipe stretches by 2 everywhere. Call that its stretch factor. Because it stretches the same amount everywhere, two different inputs can never land on the same output, and you can always undo it: subtract 1, then halve.

Compare squaring: x² sends both 2 and −2 to 4. Its stretch factor isn't the same everywhere. Near 0 it squashes everything flat, and that's where the two sides fold onto each other.

Moving to three numbers

Now take a recipe that turns three numbers (x, y, z) into three numbers. Think of a point in a room going to another point in the room. A point in a room takes three numbers to pin down, so we say the room has three dimensions; how many numbers make up a point, that's its dimension. A tiny box of starting points lands as a tiny lopsided box. Its stretch factor is how many times bigger that box's volume gets. A minus sign means it's also flipped, like a mirror image. Mathematicians call this number the Jacobian determinant.

We only allow recipes built from adding, subtracting, multiplying and whole-number powers, like 2x − 3x²y. Written out, that's 2 × x − 3 × x × x × y: in this notation xy means x × y, and x² means x × x. With x = 1 and y = 2 it's 2 − 6 = −4. Recipes like this are called polynomials.

Keller's guess (1939)

Ott-Heinrich Keller asked, in 1939:

If a polynomial recipe has the same stretch factor everywhere, and it isn't zero, can it ever send two different points to the same place? The guess: no. It can always be undone by another polynomial recipe.

With one number, the guess is true: the only polynomials with the same stretch factor everywhere are straight lines like 2x + 1. With two or more numbers, it stayed unsettled from 1939 until 2026.

What was found

On 20 July 2026, Levent Alpöge posted a recipe in three numbers. The Isabelle write-up says the announcement credits Akhil Mathew with prompting the question and the AI system Claude Fable with work leading to the map. Here it is, complete:

first  = (1 + xy)³·z + y²·(1 + xy)·(4 + 3xy)
second = y + 3x·(1 + xy)²·z + 3xy²·(4 + 3xy)
third  = 2x − 3x²y − x³z

Its stretch factor is −2 at every point: every tiny box doubles in volume and comes out flipped. Yet three different points all land in the same place:

(0, 0, −1/4)        → (−1/4, 0, 0)
(1, −3/2, 13/2)   → (−1/4, 0, 0)
(−1, 3/2, 13/2)   → (−1/4, 0, 0)

So Keller's guess is false in dimension 3. Adding extra numbers that the recipe leaves untouched gives a counterexample in every dimension above 3 too.

Within a day, others had checked it by computer: two Lean versions (Dean Cureton's and Alex Harrison's) and an Isabelle version (Freitas Ramos, Hulak and de Queiroz). Lean and Isabelle are programming languages for proofs, where a small trusted program checks every step.

Not yet in a peer-reviewed journal, and we found no full write-up from Alpöge yet. We couldn't read his post itself, so the map here comes from the three computer checks, which write it identically.

Why it matters

Finding was hard, checking is easy. Like Euler's, this is a single example anyone can check: you just checked the three points yourself. The guess stood for 87 years.

AI was credited with work leading to the map, and computers had checked it within a day.

What's still open

The two-dimensional case: a recipe that turns two numbers into two numbers. Nobody knows whether Keller's guess holds there. Also open: how the work was divided between Alpöge, Mathew and the AI, and the formal write-up.
