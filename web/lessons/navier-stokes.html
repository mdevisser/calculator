Navier–Stokes (forced version)

A Millennium Prize claim from OpenAI, made in September 2026 and not yet verified.

The question, in plain words

Water, air, honey and smoke are all fluids. In the 1800s, Claude-Louis Navier and George Stokes wrote down the rules for how a fluid moves: at every point, how fast it's going and how that speed changes from one moment to the next. Those rules are the Navier–Stokes equations. Engineers use them every day to design planes and predict weather.

Here's the puzzle. Start with a smooth, gentle flow, like a slowly stirred cup of tea. Does it stay smooth forever? Or can the swirls tighten so fast that, at some moment, the speed at one point becomes infinite? Mathematicians call that a blow-up. If that happens, the equations stop giving a sensible answer at that point.

The Millennium Prize

In 2000 the Clay Mathematics Institute offered $1 million for settling this, one of seven "Millennium Prize" problems. The official statement allows four ways to win:

  • Show it always stays smooth, with nothing pushing on the fluid (two versions: open space and a repeating box). These are statements A and B.
  • Show it can break down. Here a smooth outside push is allowed: a force, like a gentle fan or a spoon that stirs for a while and then stops. These are statements C and D.

"Forced" just means that outside push is part of the setup.

Why it's hard

Energy in a fluid can pour into smaller and smaller swirls. A computer can follow swirls down to some size, but in a blow-up the action keeps shrinking below any size a computer can follow. So you can't just simulate it: you need an argument that covers every possible flow, or builds one exact flow that provably breaks.

What was claimed

On 8 September 2026, OpenAI published a 165-page paper, Finite Time Blowup for Navier–Stokes, produced with an unreleased internal AI model. It claims to build a fluid that starts completely still, is pushed by a smooth force that acts only in a limited region and for a limited time, and reaches infinite speed in a finite time, while its total energy stays bounded. It claims this holds for every thickness of fluid (how thick or sticky it is, like honey versus water), and that it settles statements C and D: the forced versions.

OpenAI also published a version written in Lean, a programming language for proofs where a small trusted program checks every step, and a separate claim about the Euler equations (a fluid with no thickness at all), with no force.

Without a push, the question (statements A and B) is untouched by this claim and still completely open.

Who got there first is disputed

On the evening of 7 September, hours before OpenAI's announcement, mathematicians Tristan Buckmaster (NYU) and Levent Alpöge made public their own results: blow-ups with a smooth force for three related fluid equations, including the 3D Euler equations. Buckmaster's statement says they had the Boussinesq and Euler results on 15 August and checked them in Lean on 22 August, credits the basic idea to Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, and says they used several AI models, including OpenAI's own Codex.

Buckmaster says Alpöge was tipped that news of their progress had been passed to OpenAI. OpenAI says its work is independent and that his use of its tools could not have influenced its result. Both sides are still telling different stories.

Can anyone check it yet?

Not yet. Unlike Euler's equation, there's no single example you can add up.

The Lean version helps, but it isn't the end. Lean confirms that every step of the computer version follows from the last. But Lean only checks what was typed into it. On 6 October, Bastounis, Circelli and Hansen argued that OpenAI's Lean version does not correspond to its written proof, so the Lean check doesn't vouch for the paper. Whether the Lean statement says exactly what the paper and the prize mean is a job for human experts.

Experts need time. It's 165 pages of new argument. The Clay Institute's rules say a solution is considered only after it's published, at least two years have passed, and the mathematical community generally accepts it. On 11 September the Institute acknowledged "the announcement that the Navier-Stokes problem has apparently been settled", and said its process "is deliberately unhurried".

Why it matters

It's a Millennium Prize problem. Only one of the seven (the Poincaré conjecture) has ever been accepted as resolved. If this claim holds up, an AI system will have produced the core of an argument at that level.

It's a test of how we trust AI proofs. A published critique argues that the Lean check and the written paper don't correspond, and that gap is exactly what experts are now arguing about.

What's still open

Whether the claim is right. Whether the Lean version matches the written argument. Who deserves credit. And the version most people mean when they say "Navier–Stokes": with no push at all, does a smooth flow always stay smooth?
