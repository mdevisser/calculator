Sendov's conjecture

A 68-year-old guess about roots and flat spots, now proved with AI and checked by computer.

The question, in plain words

Start with a simple recipe for turning a number into another number: square it, then subtract 1. Mathematicians write that as p(x) = x² − 1. Feed it 3 and you get 3 × 3 − 1 = 8.

A root is a number the recipe turns into zero. Here there are two: 1 (because 1 × 1 − 1 = 0) and −1 (because −1 × −1 − 1 = 0).

Draw the recipe as a curve and it dips below the ground between those two roots, then comes back up. Somewhere in between, at 0, it is perfectly flat: the bottom of the valley. That isn't luck. Between any two roots there is always a flat spot. That has been known since the 1690s.

Moving from a line to a map

Mathematicians also use numbers that are points on a flat map, not just on a line. (They're called complex numbers; you won't need anything about them except that each one is a point.) A recipe like x² − 1 still has roots on the map, and it still has flat spots: points where the recipe stops changing for a moment.

Sendov's guess (around 1958)

Blagovest Sendov, a Bulgarian mathematician, guessed:

Put all of a recipe's roots inside a circle of radius 1. Then every root has a flat spot within distance 1 of it.

Our example sits right on the edge: roots at 1 and −1, flat spot at 0, and each root is exactly 1 away. So the guess, if true, can't be improved.

Why nobody could settle it

It's a claim about every recipe, with any number of roots, placed anywhere in the circle. Mathematicians proved it for recipes with up to 8 roots, and in 2020 Terence Tao proved it for recipes with a very large number of roots. Everything in between stayed open.

What was found

In early August 2026, Lech Mazur posted A Computer-Assisted Proof of Sendov's Conjecture (dated 5 August), covering every recipe with 2 or more roots. His paper says the AI system OpenAI GPT-5.6 Pro played "a substantial role in the discovery and derivation of the mathematics". Mazur directed it, chose and reconciled its output, and takes responsibility for the claims.

The proof was also written in Lean, a programming language for proofs. In Lean, every single step has to be justified, and a small, trusted checking program confirms the whole thing. If even one step is missing, it refuses.

On 12 August, Terence Tao, one of the world's leading mathematicians, published a "digestion" of the proof. He simplified it, proved a slightly stronger statement, and re-checked it in Lean with an AI agent's help: about 15,000 lines, down from about 90,000. His Lean version states Sendov's conjecture in full, with no gaps left to fill.

Not yet in a peer-reviewed journal. Mazur notes that the Lean check proves the theorem but doesn't follow his written paper line by line.

    Why it matters

    It's a proof, not a counterexample. Euler's guess fell to a single example anyone can check. Here there's no example to point at: the claim covers infinitely many cases, and the trust comes from the computer check and from Tao's reading.

    An AI did a large share of the mathematics. A person directed it and takes responsibility for it, but the paper says the AI's part went well beyond tidying up the writing.

    What's still open

    The result hasn't been through peer review yet. Tao's digestion is the first step to understanding why it's true, not just that it is, and that is what the key idea above is for.
