Euler's sum of powers

Four big numbers that broke a 200-year-old guess.

The question, in plain words

A cube is a number multiplied by itself three times: 3³ = 3 × 3 × 3 = 27. Here are three cubes that add up to another cube:

3³ + 4³ + 5³ = 6³  →  27 + 64 + 125 = 216

But you can never do it with just two cubes. That's proven: it's the smallest case of Fermat's Last Theorem.

Euler's guess (1769)

Leonhard Euler, one of the greatest mathematicians ever, saw a pattern and guessed:

To make a 3rd power out of other 3rd powers, you need at least 3 of them.
To make a 4th power, you need at least 4.
To make a 5th power, you need at least 5. And so on, forever.

Why nobody could settle it

Proving it true means showing it holds for every power and every choice of numbers. That's infinitely many cases, and nobody had a technique that could cover them. Even the two-number version (Fermat) took until 1995.

Proving it false needs just one example that breaks it, but the numbers get huge fast. 144⁵ is about 62 billion, and just the ways to pick four numbers under 150 come to about 20 million. That's hopeless by hand.

What was found

In 1966, L. J. Lander and T. R. Parkin set a CDC 6600, the fastest computer of its day, to grind through the combinations. It found:

27⁵ + 84⁵ + 110⁵ + 133⁵ = 144⁵ = 61,917,364,224

That's four fifth powers, not five, so Euler was wrong. Their paper was famously about two sentences long. Finding it took a computer; checking it takes one addition.

Try it in a spreadsheet

Open Excel, Numbers or Google Sheets and type these in exactly.

Part 1 · the 5th-power answer works.

A1: =27^5
A2: =84^5
A3: =110^5
A4: =133^5
A5: =SUM(A1:A4)  ← the four added up
A6: =144^5
A7: =A5-A6  ← should be 0

A7 shows 0: both sides are exactly 61,917,364,224. These numbers have 11 digits, small enough for a spreadsheet to keep every one.

Part 2 · the trap. Now try a number the size of the 4th-power answer, and ask a question any child can answer: a number plus 1, minus that same number, is what?

B1: =422481^4
B2: =B1+1-B1  ← obviously 1

B2 shows 0. B1 has 23 digits, but a spreadsheet keeps only about 15. The “+1” falls off the end, and nothing warns you.

Small numbers: the spreadsheet is right. Big numbers: it's confidently wrong. A rounding tool can say two things are equal when they aren't, which would be a fake counterexample. That's why this workbench never rounds when it checks.

We tested the same arithmetic spreadsheets use (in Node), not each spreadsheet app. If yours shows something different, that's interesting in its own right.

Why it matters

It's the template for 2026. The new AI results work exactly like this: finding the example is the hard part, and checking it is easy enough for anyone.

A great mind's pattern can be wrong. Euler was guessing from small cases. The counterexample only lives among much bigger numbers.

What's still open

The 4th-power case fell next. In 1986 Noam Elkies used deep theory to know where to look, because brute force couldn't reach numbers that big (published 1988). For 6th powers and higher, nobody knows yet whether Euler's guess survives.
